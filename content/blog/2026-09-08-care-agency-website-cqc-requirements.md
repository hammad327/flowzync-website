A regulated care website has two audiences that want different things, and most providers build for neither. The regulator wants to see who you are, what you are registered to do and how someone complains. A family wants to know what it costs, whether you cover their street and how soon someone can start.

This guide concerns website planning for care providers in England. CQC is not the regulator for every UK jurisdiction. Check the current rules for your service and obtain compliance advice where needed; the practical family-facing suggestions below are not all statutory requirements.

## What needs to be visible for the regulatory side

**Your registered name and address.** Not just the trading name. If you trade as "Bluebell Care" but are registered as "Bluebell Care Services Ltd", both belong on the site, usually in the footer and on the contact page. A family checking you on the CQC register needs to be able to match you.

**What you are registered to provide.** "Personal care" is a regulated activity with a specific meaning. If you are registered for personal care, say so. If you also offer companionship and domestic help that fall outside regulation, it is worth separating them, because a family does not know where the line is and the distinction affects what they are buying.

**Your current rating and the applicable display requirements.** For services covered by Regulation 20A in England, CQC requires display within 21 calendar days of publication, including on the website. Use its current service-specific guidance or widget, link to the correct service record and avoid selective or outdated ratings. If your service has not been rated, explain its actual status. Do not imply that a rating is optional where the requirement applies.

**Your complaints procedure.** It needs to be reachable, not buried. A link in the footer is enough.

**A named registered manager.** Families and inspectors both look for one. It also happens to be the single most reassuring thing on most care websites, because it turns an organisation into a person.

## What families look for that no regulator mentions

**What it costs.** This is the first question and it is almost never answered. Nobody expects a fixed hourly figure covering every care need — rates vary by package, by time of day, by whether a visit is thirty minutes or a live-in arrangement. But a range, with an explanation of what moves it, prevents the call that exists purely to establish whether you are in the right bracket.

**Whether you cover their address.** A county name is not coverage. Towns, boroughs or postcode districts are. Families discount providers whose coverage they cannot confirm in ten seconds, because the alternative is a phone call that might end in "sorry, not that far out".

**How quickly care can start.** Most enquiries come from a family in the middle of something — a hospital discharge, a fall, a diagnosis. "We can usually assess within 48 hours and start within a week" answers the question behind the question.

**Whether carers are employed or self-employed.** Families increasingly ask. Employed carers suggest training, supervision and cover arrangements. If yours are employed, that is a genuine differentiator and it is worth a sentence.

**Continuity.** Will the same carer come each time? Nothing matters more to a family and almost nobody addresses it. If you cannot promise one carer, explain the small-team approach you do use. An honest answer beats silence.

**What happens if a carer cannot make it.** Illness, traffic, emergencies. Families imagine their relative sitting alone at eight in the morning wondering where everyone is. Describe the cover arrangement.

## The things that quietly cost you enquiries

**A contact form as the only route.** A proportion of your audience is eighty years old. Put a phone number in the header, make it a tappable link on mobile, and say what hours it is answered.

**Stock photography of models.** Care is bought on trust, and a family can spot a stock image. Photographs of your actual team, even imperfect ones, do more work.

**Twelve-point grey text.** Your readers' eyesight is not the average reader's eyesight. Larger body type and proper contrast are not design preferences in this sector — they decide whether the page gets read at all.

**No clear next step.** "Contact us" is not a next step. "Book a free home care assessment" is, because it tells the family what will actually happen.

## A practical order to do this in

Start with coverage and price range, because those are the two questions that end enquiries before they begin. Then the registered details and complaints route, which take an afternoon. Then continuity and cover, which need a conversation internally before they go on the page. Then the photographs, which take longest and get put off indefinitely unless they are booked.

None of this is a redesign. Most of it is writing down answers your office already gives on the phone ten times a week.
