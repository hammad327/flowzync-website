Your website is usually the first impression anyone gets of your business — and impressions form in about **50 milliseconds**. So how do you know when it's time for more than a tweak?

## 1. It looks fine on desktop, awkward on mobile

Most of your visitors are on a phone. If your site was designed desktop-first and "adapted" to mobile, the experience usually shows it: cramped buttons, giant images, text that requires pinching. A modern rebuild starts from the small screen — it's the first principle of our [custom website design](/services/custom-website-design) process.

## 2. Loading feels like waiting

If your pages take more than three seconds to load, a large share of visitors leave before seeing anything. Speed is a design decision, not just a hosting one — image handling, code weight and animation all play a part.

## 3. You're embarrassed to share the link

This one is simple: if you hesitate before putting your URL on a proposal or business card, your website is costing you confidence — and probably credibility with the people you send it to.

## 4. Updating anything requires a developer

A healthy website lets your team change text, swap images and publish articles without touching code. If every small edit becomes a ticket, the foundation is wrong.

## 5. It doesn't reflect who you are anymore

Businesses evolve. If your services, positioning or audience have changed since launch, your website is describing a company that no longer exists.

## 6. The design could belong to anyone

Template-based sites tend to blur together. If you could swap your logo for a competitor's and nothing would feel off, the design isn't doing its job.

## 7. Visitors arrive, then vanish

High bounce rates on key pages usually mean a mismatch between what people expected and what they found — a message problem, a layout problem, or both.

## Refresh or rebuild?

A **refresh** (new imagery, updated copy, small layout fixes) makes sense when the foundation — structure, speed, mobile experience — is solid. A **rebuild** is the honest answer when the problems live in the foundation itself.

> The most expensive website is the one that quietly turns visitors away every day while looking "fine enough" to ignore.
