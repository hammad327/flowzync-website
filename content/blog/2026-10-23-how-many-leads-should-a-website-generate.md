There is no honest benchmark. Anyone quoting one without asking about your traffic sources, your sector and your price point is making it up.

A site getting 200 visitors a month, most of them people who already know the business name, behaves nothing like a site getting 2,000 from informational blog searches. The same enquiry count means something completely different in each.

What can be answered is whether *your* site is working, and that needs three numbers rather than one.

## The three numbers

**Enquiries per month.** Every route: forms, calls from the site, WhatsApp, email clicks. Most businesses undercount badly because the phone calls are invisible. If you only count form submissions, you are measuring one channel and calling it the total.

**Conversion rate.** Enquiries divided by visitors. This is the one that tells you whether the site itself is doing its job, because it removes traffic volume from the question. A site with falling traffic but a steady conversion rate has a visibility problem. A site with rising traffic and a falling conversion rate has a page problem or is attracting the wrong people.

**Close rate.** Enquiries that become customers. If this is poor, more enquiries will not help — you are attracting the wrong ones, and the fix is usually on the page, in what it says about price, scope and who you are for.

Together they locate the problem. Separately, each can mislead completely.

## Why conversion rate benchmarks are nearly useless

Intent decides almost everything.

Someone searching your business name is close to enquiring. Someone searching "emergency plumber Leeds" is close. Someone searching "how much does a bathroom cost" is researching and might buy in three months. Someone who landed on a blog post about tap types is not buying anything today.

A site with mostly branded and commercial traffic can convert at five percent or better. A site whose traffic is mostly informational blog visits might convert at half a percent and be perfectly healthy — the blog is doing a different job, which is being found at all and being remembered later.

Averaging them produces a number that describes neither.

So segment before judging. Look at conversion from your service pages separately from your blog. The service page number is the one that tells you whether the site sells.

## What a thin number usually means

**Traffic landing in the wrong place.** Check which pages receive the first visit. If almost everything lands on blog posts, the site is working as a publication and not as a shop, and the fix is service pages that can rank rather than more posts.

**The page does not answer the pre-enquiry questions.** People do not enquire while uncertain about price range, coverage area, timescale or whether you handle their particular situation. Every unanswered question is a reason to check the next result instead. Most small business sites answer none of the four.

**No obvious next step.** "Contact us" is not a next step — it describes a mechanism, not an outcome. "Book a free site visit" or "Get an itemised quote" tells someone what will happen.

**The form asks too much.** Every field loses people. Name, contact, and a sentence about the job is enough to start a conversation; the rest can be asked on the phone.

**Nothing proves you exist properly.** Real photographs of real work, named people, reviews, an address. Trust is the gate, and a stock-photo site with no evidence does not pass it.

**No route for people who will not fill in forms.** A sizeable share will ring instead, and some will only use WhatsApp. If the phone number is not in the header and tappable on mobile, that group is simply lost.

## When traffic is the real problem

If the conversion rate is reasonable and enquiries are still too few, the site is converting what it gets and there is not enough of it. That is a visibility problem and no amount of button colour changes will touch it.

That work is different: pages for the services and places you want to be found for, a Google Business Profile doing its job, and reviews. Slower, and it compounds.

The common mistake is spending months on conversion tweaks when the site receives eighty visitors a month. At that volume you cannot even measure whether a change helped.

## The number that actually matters

Not leads. Revenue from the website, against what the website cost.

Twenty enquiries a month that mostly waste your time is a worse outcome than six that mostly convert. A site that ranks for price-shopping searches will produce the former and feel busy while doing it.

If you track one thing, track where your actual customers found you. Ask every one. It is unscientific, it takes ten seconds, and it is more useful than any analytics dashboard for deciding what to do next.
