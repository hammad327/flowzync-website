Most care providers do not have a CRM problem. They have a Friday evening problem.

An enquiry arrives at six on Friday. It sits in a shared inbox. On Monday someone sees it and rings, and the family signed with another provider on Saturday. Nothing was mishandled. The process simply had a gap in it that nobody could see.

A CRM is the thing that closes that gap. Whether you need one, and which one, comes down to a few specifics that generic CRM advice misses entirely.

## First, the distinction that matters

**Care management software** runs the service you deliver: rostering, care plans, eMAR, visit verification, timesheets, invoicing. Access, Birdie, CareLineLive, Nourish and the rest live here.

**A CRM** runs everything before someone becomes a client or an employee: enquiries, applications, follow-up, conversion.

They are different products solving different problems, and most providers have the first and not the second. Any vendor claiming one tool does both well is overstating it — and the overlap they are describing is usually a basic enquiry list bolted onto a rostering system.

## What a care CRM needs that a sales CRM does not

**Two pipelines, properly separate.** Family enquiries and carer applications are both pipelines, and they could not be less alike. A family is making an emotional decision under time pressure, often during a hospital discharge. An applicant is comparing employers and will take the first reasonable offer. Different stages, different timescales, different sequences, different people watching them. A single pipeline with a tag is not the same thing.

**Immediate response on more than one channel.** The highest-value feature available to a care provider, and it is not sophisticated: a form submitted at nine on a Sunday evening generates an SMS and an email within seconds saying who will be in touch and when. Families contact several providers in one sitting. The first one that responds earns disproportionate trust, and most of your competitors are not responding until Monday.

**Document chasing for recruitment.** DBS, right to work, references, training certificates. Unglamorous, and probably the highest-return automation in the whole sector, because the gap between an applicant accepting and actually starting is where recruitment pipelines leak. Automatic reminders until the document arrives.

**Enquiry source tracking that you will actually use.** Directory, Google, word of mouth, local authority. Care providers typically pay for several directories and have no reliable idea which produces clients rather than enquiries. The CRM should make that visible without a reporting project.

**Handover to the care management system.** The point where an enquiry becomes a client is a real boundary. It does not need to be a deep integration — it needs to be a clear, repeatable step so nothing gets re-keyed twice or lost in between.

## The data protection part, before you buy

A care enquiry is not an ordinary sales lead. It contains information about someone's health, which is special category data under UK GDPR, and the person it concerns is often not the person who filled in the form.

Ask the vendor, in writing:

- **Where is the data stored?** UK or EEA, or a transfer mechanism you can point to.
- **Will you sign a data processing agreement?** If this is awkward, that is the answer.
- **Can access be restricted by role?** A care coordinator needs the enquiry detail. A marketing contractor does not.
- **What is the retention and deletion behaviour?** Enquiries that never converted should not sit there for seven years. Can you actually delete, and does deletion reach backups?
- **What happens if we leave?** Export format, and how long until it is destroyed.

None of this is exotic, and a vendor who has worked with regulated providers answers it quickly. One that does not is telling you something useful.

## What actually decides whether it works

Not the software. Two other things.

**The setup.** A CRM out of the box is an expensive contact list. The pipelines have to match how enquiries genuinely reach you — including the ones that come by phone and the ones that come through a hospital discharge team rather than a form. Sequences have to be written for a family making a difficult decision, which means they cannot sound like sales emails.

**Whether the team uses it.** A system only the person who built it understands is abandoned within two months. The care coordinator or office manager who will run it every day needs a proper walkthrough, and ideally needs to have been in the room when it was designed. If they were not, it will quietly revert to the inbox and the spreadsheet.

## When not to buy one

If you take three enquiries a month and answer them within the hour, a subscription is solving a problem you do not have. Better process, a shared inbox with an owner, and a calendar reminder will cover it.

And if the problem is that not enough enquiries arrive at all, a CRM changes nothing. That is a visibility problem — your website, your Google Business Profile, local search — and the follow-up layer is worth adding once there is something to follow up.

Fix the leak when enquiries are arriving and draining away. Fix visibility when they are not arriving. The order matters, and it is the one most providers get backwards.
