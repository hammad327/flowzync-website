Website maintenance covers **updates, backups, security, uptime monitoring, performance checks and a defined amount of content editing.** For a small business site that is typically £40–£150 a month; for e-commerce or anything with real functionality, £150–£500.

The question behind the question is usually "is this worth it, or is it a subscription for doing nothing?" Fair question. Here is what actually happens, and what happens when it does not.

## What a maintenance plan should include

**Core, theme and plugin updates.** WordPress releases security patches regularly. Plugins update constantly. Applying them is straightforward; applying them *safely* is the actual work, because updates occasionally break things. That means updating on a staging copy, checking the site still works, then pushing live — not clicking "update all" on a Friday afternoon and hoping.

**Backups, off-site, tested.** Daily for e-commerce, weekly for a brochure site. Stored somewhere other than the same server, because a backup on the machine that failed is not a backup. And restored occasionally to prove they work — an untested backup is a guess.

**Security monitoring.** Malware scanning, login protection, firewall rules, file integrity checks. Most attacks on small sites are automated and indiscriminate. Nobody targeted you; a script found an out-of-date plugin.

**Uptime monitoring.** Checks every few minutes and alerts someone. Ideally that someone is not you at 2am.

**Performance checks.** Core Web Vitals drift as content is added. A quarterly look catches the slow decay before it becomes a rebuild.

**Broken link and form checks.** Forms break silently, and you find out weeks later when you notice the enquiries stopped. This alone justifies a lot of plans.

**A content allowance.** Usually one to three hours a month for text changes, new pages, image swaps. Use it or lose it, generally.

**Someone who answers.** Genuinely part of what you are buying: a defined response time when something is wrong, rather than emailing a freelancer and waiting.

## What is usually not included

Redesigns. New functionality. SEO campaigns. Content writing beyond simple edits. Recovering from a hack, which is often billed separately and is expensive. Hosting, sometimes bundled and sometimes not — check which.

Read the response time carefully too. "Next business day" and "within four hours" are very different products, and for an e-commerce site the difference is measured in lost orders.

## What actually goes wrong without it

**Getting hacked.** Overwhelmingly through an out-of-date plugin. The usual outcome is not dramatic — no defacement, just spam pages quietly injected into your site, which Google finds and flags. By the time you notice, you have lost rankings, and cleaning it costs £300–£1,500 plus weeks of recovery. Prevention is one plugin update.

**Breaking on a PHP upgrade.** Hosts upgrade PHP periodically. Sites running abandoned plugins break, sometimes entirely. Fixing it under pressure, on a site nobody has touched in two years, is a bad afternoon.

**Silent form failure.** A plugin update, a changed mailbox password, an expired API key. The form says "thank you" and the email goes nowhere. We are called about this several times a year and the enquiries lost are unrecoverable.

**Slow decay.** Nobody notices a site getting 5% slower each quarter until it fails Core Web Vitals and the rankings have already gone.

## Is it worth it?

Depends honestly on two things: what the site is worth to you, and whether you will do it yourself.

**If your site generates enquiries or sales**, maintenance is cheap insurance. One prevented hack or one caught broken form pays for a year.

**If your site is a brochure that nobody visits**, a £150/month plan is hard to justify. Something minimal — updates and backups — is enough.

**If you are technical and disciplined**, you can absolutely do this yourself. Updates on staging, off-site backups, a security plugin, an uptime monitor. Perhaps an hour a month. The word doing the work in that sentence is *disciplined*: almost everyone starts and stops, and the stopping is invisible until it is expensive.

## Questions to ask a provider

- Are updates tested on staging first, or applied straight to the live site?
- Where are backups stored, how long are they kept, and when did you last restore one?
- What is your response time, and is that business hours or actual hours?
- How many hours of content changes are included, and do they roll over?
- What happens if the site is hacked — is recovery included or billed?
- Is hosting included?
- Do I get a report, and how often?

That third question separates real plans from automated ones. Anyone can install an update plugin. Answering the phone at 6pm on a Thursday is the service.

## The minimum, if you do nothing else

Even with no plan at all: automatic off-site backups, updates applied at least monthly, and a test of your own contact form every quarter. That covers the three failures that account for most of the genuinely expensive problems.

Set a calendar reminder. It is fifteen minutes, four times a year, and it will save you a rebuild.
