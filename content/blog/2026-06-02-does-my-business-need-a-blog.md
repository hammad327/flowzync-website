**Most small businesses do not need a blog. Some absolutely do.** The difference is not the size of the business or the industry — it is whether your customers search for information before they buy, and whether you will realistically publish for at least a year.

If either answer is no, spend the effort somewhere else. A blog whose last post is eighteen months old is worse than no blog at all: it tells every visitor that nobody is minding the shop.

## When a blog genuinely earns its place

**When people research before buying.** Anything expensive, anything technical, anything where the customer needs to understand options before choosing. Someone spending £8,000 on a bathroom reads for weeks first. Someone buying a sandwich does not.

**When you can answer questions nobody else answers properly.** The best-performing posts are almost always the ones where you explain something the industry prefers to keep vague — real costs, what actually goes wrong, how to tell a good supplier from a bad one. It feels risky. It works because it is the thing people are actually searching for.

**When your service pages cannot cover everything.** A service page has one job and should not be forty questions long. The questions go on their own pages and link back.

**When AI assistants are quoting your competitors.** Increasingly, people ask ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or Perplexity a question rather than searching. Those systems quote sources that state things clearly and specifically. If nobody in your sector is publishing plainly written, factual answers, that space is genuinely open right now — and it is cheaper to occupy than the equivalent position in Google was ten years ago.

## When it is a waste of your time

**When your customers do not research.** Emergency plumbing, locksmiths, takeaway food. Nobody reads a blog post at 11pm with water coming through the ceiling. Put that effort into your Google Business Profile, your reviews, and how fast you answer the phone.

**When you will not sustain it.** Four posts and then silence is a cost, not an asset. Be honest about this before you start rather than four months in.

**When your service pages are still weak.** Fix those first. A blog post that ranks and sends people to a bad service page has helped nobody.

**When you are outsourcing it to someone who does not know your trade.** Generic outsourced content is obvious to readers and increasingly discounted by search engines. Ten of them will not outperform one post you wrote yourself about something you actually know.

## What "doing it properly" actually means

Fewer posts than you think, better than you think.

**Four to twelve posts a year, done well.** One genuinely good post a month beats four thin ones a week, and it is a schedule a real business can keep.

**One question per post.** Titled the way someone would actually search it. "How much does a bathroom installation cost in Leeds" is a title. "Bathroom Trends 2026" is not — nobody is searching for that.

**Answer in the first paragraph.** Do not warm up. The opening is what gets pulled into a Google answer box or quoted by an AI assistant, and it is what a reader uses to decide whether to keep going.

**Be specific and be willing to give numbers.** Vague content ranks for nothing and gets quoted by nobody. Specific content is quotable, and quotable is the whole game now.

**Link to the page that makes you money.** Every post should point somewhere — a service, an industry page, a contact form. A post with no internal links is a dead end.

**Update the good ones.** A post from two years ago that still gets traffic is worth an hour of refreshing far more than a new post is worth writing.

## How long before it does anything

Six to twelve months before a blog produces meaningful traffic, on a site with some existing authority. Longer on a brand new domain. The first three or four posts usually do nothing at all, and that is normal rather than a sign it is not working.

This is the honest reason most business blogs fail. They are abandoned in month four, which is exactly the point at which nothing has happened yet and nothing was supposed to have happened yet.

## The test I would apply

Ask yourself two questions.

**Do my customers type questions into a search bar before they contact anyone in my industry?** If you genuinely do not know, ask the last five customers what they looked at before calling you. It is a five-minute exercise and it settles the argument.

**Will I still be publishing in twelve months?** Not "do I intend to". Will I. If the answer depends on the business being quiet enough, the answer is no.

Two yeses: start, and commit to a year. Anything else: put the time into your service pages, your Google Business Profile and your reviews. That is not a consolation prize — for a lot of local businesses it is straightforwardly the better investment.
