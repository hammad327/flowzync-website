The person reading a care agency website is almost never the person who needs care. It is their adult daughter, usually around midnight, usually after a difficult week, with three provider websites open in three tabs.

Everything on the page should be built for her. Most care websites are built for a general visitor who does not exist.

## What she is actually looking for

In roughly this order:

**Do you cover where mum lives?** If she cannot establish this in about ten seconds, she closes the tab. Coverage area belongs near the top of the homepage, in plain words, not on a contact page three clicks in.

**What does it cost?** She has no idea whether this is £20 an hour or £40, and she is trying to work out whether the conversation is even worth having. A range with a clear list of what is included answers it. "Contact us for a personalised quote" does not, and it is the most common reason someone moves to the next tab.

**Can I trust these people with my mother?** This is the real question and it is the one most sites answer worst. It is answered by a named registered manager with a photograph and a background, by staff who are described as people rather than as "our dedicated team", and by an inspection rating shown openly.

**What happens next if I get in touch?** She is bracing for a hard sell. Saying plainly that the first step is a free, no-obligation assessment, and what that involves, removes more hesitation than any amount of copy about compassion.

## The four things that lose her

**Fees hidden entirely.** She will not ring for a price at midnight. She will find a provider that published one.

**The inspection rating buried.** She is going to check the regulator anyway. Not showing it reads as concealment, and showing a good one is free credibility.

**Stock photography.** Everyone recognises it. A generic image of an anonymous smiling older person tells her nothing and quietly signals that there was nothing real to show. Photographs of your actual staff, however imperfect, do more work.

**Language that sounds like marketing.** "Person-centred care delivered with compassion and dignity" appears on almost every care website in the country. It is not wrong, it is just invisible. What she remembers is the specific thing — that you match carers to clients and try to keep the same carer, that you cover a fifteen-mile radius, that your manager has done the job for twenty years.

## What to put on the page instead

- Coverage area, stated plainly and near the top
- Hourly or weekly rates as a range, with what is and is not included
- Your current inspection rating, with the report linked
- The registered manager, named, with a photograph and their actual background
- The types of support you provide, described concretely rather than as categories
- What a first assessment involves, and that it costs nothing
- Real photographs of real staff
- A recruitment section — because she may also know someone looking for work, and because it is half of why the site exists

## The accessibility point that is not optional here

A meaningful share of visitors to a care website have a visual impairment, a tremor, or are simply reading on a phone at arm's length in poor light.

Larger base type, tested colour contrast, buttons with words rather than icons alone, and forms that explain an error instead of turning a box red — these are not a compliance checkbox in this sector. They are the audience. A site that is hard for an older visitor to use is failing at the specific thing it exists to do.

## The honest summary

Write the site for one person: a worried adult child at midnight, comparing you against two other providers, who wants to know whether you cover the area, what it costs, whether you can be trusted, and what happens if she gets in touch.

Answer those four things clearly and you will out-convert providers with far better photography.
