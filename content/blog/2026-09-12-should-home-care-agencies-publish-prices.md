Publish a range. Not a single rate, not nothing.

That is the short answer, and most of the argument against it does not survive contact with how families actually choose a care provider.

## Why most agencies do not publish

**"It depends on the care package."** True, and it is the strongest objection. An hour of companionship and an hour of complex personal care with two carers are not the same product. A single advertised rate is either misleading or has to be set high enough to cover the worst case, which loses you the simple packages.

But "it depends" is an argument against publishing *one number*. It is not an argument against publishing anything.

**"Competitors will undercut us."** This one is almost entirely imaginary. Your competitors can find your rates by ringing the office, and some of them have. Local authority framework rates are often published. Carers move between providers and talk. The only group reliably kept in the dark by a price-free website is the families trying to decide whether to call you.

**"We want the conversation first."** Understandable, and it is where a good provider adds real value — most families do not know what level of care they need. But a family who rings to ask the price and then discovers you are a third more than they can afford has not had a useful conversation. They have had an awkward one, and so have you.

**"Our price is higher and we would rather explain it."** Then explain it on the page. A higher rate with employed carers, consistent staffing, and a named care coordinator is a defensible position in writing. Hiding the number does not make the comparison go away; it just means the family makes it without your explanation attached.

## What families are actually doing

Picture the real scenario. Someone's mother came out of hospital on Friday. On Sunday evening her daughter opens six tabs and works through them.

She is not evaluating your philosophy of care. She is establishing three things: do they cover Mum's address, can they start soon, and is this remotely affordable. Anything that answers those gets a phone call on Monday. Anything that does not gets closed.

She has limited evenings and a lot going on. The providers who answered her questions are the shortlist, and the shortlist is where the decision actually happens.

## The middle option

**An hourly range for visiting care.** "Our visiting care rates start from £X an hour and typically fall between £X and £Y, depending on the length of the visit and the level of support." That is honest, useful and commits you to nothing.

**A separate weekly figure for live-in.** It is a different product bought by different people and blending it into an hourly average helps nobody.

**What moves the number.** Thirty-minute calls usually carry a higher effective hourly rate than hour-long ones. Evenings, weekends and bank holidays cost more. Two-carer calls cost roughly double. Say so. Families are not surprised by any of this, and the explanation does more for your credibility than the number does damage.

**What is included.** Travel, mileage, care planning, out-of-hours support — providers vary and families cannot tell. If your rate includes things a cheaper competitor charges separately, that comparison only works if both are visible.

**The assessment line.** "The exact figure comes after a free assessment, because it depends on what the care actually involves." This is the sentence that makes a published range safe. It frames the number as indicative, which is what it is.

## What this does for search

There is a practical side effect. "How much does home care cost in [town]" is a real search with real volume and remarkably few local providers answering it, because they all made the same decision not to publish. A page that answers it honestly can rank in a market where almost nobody is competing for the query.

It is also exactly the sort of question an AI assistant gets asked and has to answer from somewhere. If your page is the one in your area that gives a straight answer, it is the one that gets quoted.

## The one case for staying quiet

If you work almost entirely through local authority or NHS contracts and private clients are incidental, published rates serve little purpose and can complicate commissioning conversations. Everyone else is protecting information their competitors already have, from the only people who needed it.
