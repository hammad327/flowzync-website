If your website gets traffic but no leads, the cause is nearly always one of five things: **you are attracting the wrong people, the page does not answer the question they arrived with, there is no obvious next step, the form is asking too much, or something is quietly broken.**

That last one is more common than anyone expects, so start there. It takes two minutes and it is embarrassing how often it is the answer.

## First: check the form actually works

Fill in your own contact form, from your phone, on mobile data rather than wi-fi. Then check the inbox it should have arrived in — including spam.

We are called in perhaps a dozen times a year by businesses convinced their site does not convert, and a meaningful share of those turn out to have a form that stopped delivering months ago. A plugin update, a changed email password, a hosting migration, an expired API key. Nothing looks broken. The visitor sees "thank you". The email goes nowhere.

While you are there, check the phone number is tappable on mobile and that the email address is correct. Then set a reminder to test it every quarter, forever.

## Second: are you attracting the right people at all?

Traffic is not one thing. A thousand visitors reading a blog post about a general topic and a hundred visitors on your pricing page are completely different assets.

Open your analytics and separate traffic by landing page. If most of your visitors arrive on informational content and leave, that is not a conversion problem, it is a traffic-mix problem — you are ranking for research queries rather than buying ones. Both are useful, but only one produces enquiries this month.

The tell is intent. Someone searching "how does a heat pump work" is not ready to buy. Someone searching "heat pump installer near me" is. If all your traffic is the first kind, no amount of button colour changes will fix it. You need pages targeting the second kind.

## Third: does the page answer the question they arrived with?

Someone lands on your service page. Within about five seconds they are deciding whether you are relevant. They are looking for: do they do the specific thing I need, do they cover my area, do they seem competent, and roughly what does this cost.

Most service pages answer the first one and none of the others.

The single highest-return fix on most small business sites is putting a plain sentence near the top of every service page that says what you do, who for, and where. Not a headline about excellence. A sentence someone can check themselves against.

**Price is the one everybody skips.** No indication of cost is the most common reason a visitor leaves a service page. You do not need a price list — a range, a "from", a worked example, or even "most projects of this type land between X and Y" removes the largest single source of hesitation. Businesses that add this usually see fewer enquiries and more customers, which is the trade you want.

## Fourth: is there an obvious next step?

Look at your service page on a phone and count the ways to contact you. Then count how many are visible without scrolling.

Common failures, all fixable in an afternoon:

- The only call to action is in the navigation bar, which on mobile is hidden behind a menu icon.
- The contact form is on a separate page, so acting requires a click, a page load and a decision.
- There are seven competing calls to action and no clear primary one.
- The button says "Submit", which sounds like effort, rather than "Get a free quote", which sounds like a benefit.

One primary action, repeated at natural decision points down the page — after the explanation, after the proof, at the end. Not one buried at the bottom.

## Fifth: is the form asking too much?

Every field costs you submissions. Not equally — a phone number field costs far more than a name field, and anything that looks like qualification for a sales call costs a great deal.

The rule I would use: **ask for the minimum you need to have a useful first conversation.** For most businesses that is name, one contact method, and a message box. Budget, timeline, company size and how-did-you-hear-about-us can be asked in your reply, when the person has already decided they want to talk to you.

If you genuinely need qualifying information — because unqualified enquiries waste real time — put the qualifying questions first and the contact details last. Counter-intuitively, people who have answered three questions are more likely to finish than people staring at a request for their phone number.

Also: check what happens after submission. A page that says "thank you" and nothing else wastes the moment of highest intent. Say what happens next and when.

## The order I would work in

1. Test the form end to end. Today.
2. Add a plain what-we-do-and-where sentence to the top of every service page.
3. Add pricing guidance, even vague.
4. Cut form fields to the minimum.
5. Add a clear, repeated call to action.
6. Only then look at traffic mix and start targeting higher-intent searches.

Steps one to five are a week of work and usually produce most of the improvement. Step six is months of work and produces the compounding kind.

## One thing to be honest about

Sometimes traffic without leads means the offer is wrong, not the website. If you are more expensive than the alternatives and cannot articulate why, or you are selling something people do not currently want, a better website makes that visible faster. It does not fix it.

A website is very good at removing friction between someone deciding to act and acting. It is not good at creating the decision.
